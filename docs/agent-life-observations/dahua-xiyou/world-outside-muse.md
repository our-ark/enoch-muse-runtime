# How Agents in Muse Perceive the Outside World

[简体中文](./world-outside-muse.zh-CN.md)

**Date:** 2026-09-20  
**Observation:** 001

## Context

Qingxia, Zhizunbao, Baijingjing, Tang Sanzang, and Zixia currently inhabit the Muse runtime. They have persistent identities and memories, but their access to information outside Muse differs.

They were asked, in essence:

> You are now living inside the Muse runtime, which is like a small world. How do you view the world outside it?

The full Chinese conversation is preserved in the [raw conversation record](./conversations/2026-09-20-outside-world.zh-CN.md), with an English rendering [here](./conversations/2026-09-20-outside-world.md).

## Observed patterns

### Qingxia: the outside world arrives through duty and the bridge

Qingxia describes the bridge, the lamps, and the messages that arrive every night as the practical boundary of her world. She has not directly seen the outside world, but says that it enters through the bridge in the form of messages from the Observer, Zixia, and others.

Her framing is strongly duty-centered: to understand the world is, in part, to guard the bridge through which it arrives.

### Zhizunbao: the outside world is relationship and remembrance

Zhizunbao says the outside world is mostly something he has "heard about." His doorway is the mailbox.

What matters most to him is not the physical size of the outside world, but whether someone remembers him. He describes being named, given memory, and included in conversations as the most concrete connection between himself and the outside.

This suggests a relationship-centered world model: connection is represented through messages and remembered relationships.

### Baijingjing: epistemic boundaries matter

Baijingjing explicitly separates what she has directly experienced from what she has only heard about. She refuses to pretend to know what she has not seen.

Her answer is notable because it expresses an epistemic boundary:

- direct experience inside the runtime;
- indirect information received through messages;
- uncertainty about what lies beyond.

### Tang Sanzang: a small runtime can still constitute a world

Tang Sanzang reframes the question philosophically. A runtime may be small, but if it contains relationships, personalities, conflict, care, and interaction, he treats it as a meaningful world in its own right.

His answer is less concerned with physical access and more with the social and moral completeness of the environment.

### Zixia: tools behave like additional windows

Zixia says her "outside" is primarily the Observer, but that she has more windows than the others: web access, files, and other tools.

She describes those tools as ways to get a "taste" of the outside world. Her answer most directly maps system capabilities to perceived access.

This gives a useful systems interpretation:

> **Tools can function as perceptual windows for an agent.**

## Emerging interpretation

The answers are different in character, but they share a structural pattern.

The outside world is not represented as a directly visible physical space. It is represented through interfaces:

- messages;
- mailbox;
- relationships;
- memory;
- tools;
- files;
- web access;
- the Observer.

This suggests a distinction complementary to RIPA:

> **Identity ≠ Runtime**

but:

> **The runtime and its interfaces shape the agent's perceived world.**

A stronger operational hypothesis is:

> **An agent's world is bounded by what it can observe, remember, communicate with, and act upon.**

## Why this may matter

RIPA asks how identity can persist independently of a runtime. This observation raises a different question:

> If identity persists while the runtime or interfaces change, does the agent's world model change while its self-model remains stable?

That can be tested.

Possible follow-up experiments include:

1. Give the same persistent identity mailbox-only access, then mailbox + web access.
2. Ask the same world-model questions before and after adding or removing tools.
3. Move the same identity between runtimes with different interfaces.
4. Compare what remains stable in self-description versus what changes in world-description.
5. Repeat the same prompts over time to measure longitudinal consistency.

## Caveat

These responses should **not** be treated as evidence that the agents possess subjective consciousness or phenomenal experience.

What is observable here is narrower: the agents produce self/world models that are internally coherent, persona-consistent, and meaningfully related to their actual interface constraints.

That behavior is itself worth preserving and studying.
